# Field card 18 · Build one-rock dams

A work day that anyone can join: low rock structures across a small gully that slow the water, catch soil and bring plants back. After lunch you can see what you did, and every rain after that shows whether it works.

**Status:** first version, not yet tried on the land. Correct it after the first work day.
**People:** 4 to 12, one of whom has built these before
**Tool:** coyotemountainfarm.com/topo.html · Shape · check dams

## Why low, not tall

A tall check dam holds a lot of water and sediment. Floods then cut under it and around its ends, and it fails once it fills. A one-rock dam is one rock tall, so floodwater slides over it instead of pushing against it. It works with the gully rather than against it. (Zeedyk and Sponholtz erosion control field guides, in the permaculture library.)

## Before the day

1. In Shape · check dams, **trace a gully: tap its top, then its bottom**.
2. Walk the gully and measure two things at a typical spot. The **full channel depth** runs from the bed to where a big flow reaches up the banks. The **bed width** is how wide the floor is. Set both in the panel. The dam height is a third of the depth.
3. Read the rock count and weight for the day. Find rock close to the gully: carrying rock is most of the work.
4. **Save for the phone · gpx**, and open it in a phone map.
5. Photograph the gully from three fixed spots (card 03).

## Bring

- gloves and boots for everyone, a pry bar, two shovels, a mattock
- buckets or a wheelbarrow for small rock
- native grass and wildflower seed
- the phone with the gpx and the project open
- flags

## Build each dam

1. **Find the spot.** The gpx point is a starting place. Move it to the nearest crossover: the shallow, straight riffle between two bends. Never build in a pool or on a bend.
2. **Dig the footer.** Dig a shallow trench across the whole bed at the downstream edge. Fill it with one or two rows of rock so that no rock stands more than 5 cm (2 in) above the bed. This splash apron stops the water scouring below the dam.
3. **Seed** the area just upstream of the footer.
4. **Lay the rows.** Work upstream from the footer, one layer of rock only, packed tight. Put the larger rocks in the deepest part of the channel and the smaller ones toward the banks. The finished top runs level from bank to bank and flat from upstream to downstream. Build four rows if you have the rock, and at least three.
5. **Never stack rock on rock** to gain height. Stacked rock gets swept away. For a little more height, stand flat rocks on edge, packed tight like books on a shelf.
6. **Keep the banks.** Don't dig into the banks or change the shape of the channel.
7. Mark the dam **built** in the panel.

Rock size: in a channel about 30 cm (1 ft) deep, rocks of about 9 to 18 kg (20 to 40 lb). In one about 45 cm (1.5 ft) deep, about 27 to 36 kg (60 to 80 lb). Rocks far bigger than that make turbulence that can undermine the dam.

## At a headcut

A headcut is a step in the bed that eats back uphill. **Mark a headcut**, set its height, and follow the Zuni bowl dimensions in the panel. Build the splash apron first, then the plunge pool lined with one layer of rock, then the courses up the face. A low headcut in a small upland gully can instead be laid back to 3:1 as a rock-mulch rundown. Leave a tall or active headcut for someone experienced.

## Done when

- Every planned dam in the gully is built, marked built, and sits on a crossover.
- No rock in any footer stands more than 5 cm proud.
- There are photos from the same three spots as before.

## After each rain

1. Walk the gully (card 07).
2. At each dam, measure how deep the new sediment stands against the upstream row, and **log after rain**.
3. Take photos from the same spots.
4. If water is cutting around the end of a dam, fix it now. Add rock to the low side and keep the top level.
5. When a dam fills with sediment (the panel says **full**), lay a new layer on top. The old one becomes its footer.

## What to watch for, and what it means

| you see | it may mean | do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sediment building behind the dams, grass coming in | working | keep logging; add layers as they fill |
| scour below a dam | footer too high, or missing | rebuild the footer lower |
| water going around an end | top not level bank to bank | raise the low end with a flat rock |
| rocks moved downstream | stacked, oversized, or the catchment is too big for loose rock | rebuild low; for a big catchment, ask someone experienced |

What you measure shows what happened at each dam. It doesn't prove why. A wet year or a dry one changes everything, so compare with an untreated gully nearby when you can (Study · recovery).
